# Frame control & status (Klaff, *Pitch Anything*, 2011)

Rules translated from Klaff's in-person pitch method to written channels —
pitch decks (`deck`), pitch/outreach emails (`email`), one-pagers and public
posts (`post`).

**Read the evidence frame first:** Klaff's "croc brain" rests on the
triune-brain model, which modern neuroscience treats as discredited (§7). The
durable, defensible kernel — supported by dual-process research on attention
and affect — is: attention is scarce, first impressions are fast and
affect-driven, and complexity loses readers before argument begins. The rules
below operationalize that kernel; treat "croc brain" as Klaff's label for it,
not as a working brain model. The most transferable subset is the *negative*
rule set: kill neediness, kill supplication, kill over-explaining.

## 1. The fast attention filter — write for the skimming first pass

The reader's first pass is fast, affect-driven, threat-avoidant, and
novelty-seeking; it ignores anything abstract, boring, or complicated. Only
what survives it gets analytical attention. You compose with full attention;
the reader receives with almost none.

- **Open with something concrete and novel, never with context-setting or credentials.** [deck/email/post] The filter discards the familiar; a throat-clearing opener gets the whole message binned.
- **Cut jargon and abstraction from the first screen/slide; use plain nouns and pictures.** [deck/email] Complexity reads as threat and gets summarized away — your nuance never reaches the reader.
- **One big idea per pitch; state it in the first 2–3 sentences (email) or slide 1–2 (deck).** A multi-idea pitch registers as noise.
- **Make the email skimmable in under 30 seconds; make the deck's core arc land in ~10–12 slides.** Length past the attention window signals neediness, not thoroughness.
- **Remove anything that could read as a threat to the reader's status or budget in the opening** (hard asks, guilt, pressure). Threat triggers archive-before-evaluate.

## 2. Frame control — who sets the agenda of the document

Every encounter is a collision of frames; the stronger frame absorbs the weaker. "If you have to explain your authority, power, position, or leverage, you do not hold the stronger frame."

- **Power frame → set the agenda yourself.** [email/deck] Open by stating what the document covers, what you're deciding, and what happens next — never "hoping to get on your radar." Never sycophancy toward titles.
- **Time frame → impose your own limit.** [email/deck] "This takes 3 minutes to read; I'm closing conversations Friday" rather than "whenever you get a chance." Whoever owns the clock owns the frame; open-ended availability is a weak frame.
- **Analyst frame → intrigue frame: separate detail from narrative.** [deck/email] Spreadsheets and data rooms go in an appendix; the pitch body stays narrative plus headline numbers. Hot and cold cognition can't run simultaneously — detail-grinding kills desire. Answer written diligence questions briefly, then return to the story.
- **Never argue your way back into a frame with more facts, longer explanations, or more attachments.** Over-explaining is itself the tell of the weaker frame.
- **Checkable test: who is auditioning for whom?** If your text answers their implicit questions on their structure ("as requested…" + apology + flexibility), they own the frame. If it states your structure, your criteria, and your timeline, you do.

## 3. Status and prizing — be the prize, ban supplication

Neediness is "the number one deal killer." Prizing means reframing so the reader must qualify to *you* — make the money chase the deal.

**Banned supplicating phrases** (greppable):

- "just checking in" / "just following up" / "just wanted to…"
- "any chance you could…" / "would it be at all possible…"
- "sorry to bother you" / "I know you're busy, but…"
- "whenever you get a chance" / "no rush at all" / "at your convenience"
- "I'd love the opportunity to…" / "it would mean a lot"
- "thank you so much for your time" (as opener — time-thanking is frame surrender)
- "hoping to pick your brain" / "even 15 minutes would be amazing"
- "does this make sense?" / "let me know your thoughts!" (validation-seeking closers)
- "no worries if not"

(The Vale `Marketing.Supplication` rule enforces a greppable subset of this
list; position-dependent phrases like time-thanking openers stay a manual
check.)

Positive rules:

- **Replace open-ended pleading closers with a decision fork you own.** "If this fits your thesis, reply and I'll send the data room; if not, no need to respond — I'm closing conversations on the 22nd."
- **State qualification criteria for *them*.** [deck/email] "We're choosing two partners who can X" flips who is being evaluated.
- **Cap credentials at 2–3 lines of track record — deeds, not adjectives — then move on.** Dwelling on credentials is explaining your authority, proof you don't hold the frame.
- **Signal scarcity of your attention truthfully** (parallel conversations, a closing date, limited slots) — never fabricate it; fake scarcity, once detected, collapses status permanently.
- **Each follow-up must add new information** (a milestone, a new fact, a deadline), never merely re-ask. A contentless bump is pure validation-seeking; a status update from a busy principal is prizing.
- **Eradicate wanting on the page: write as if you'd be fine walking away.** No begging, no over-availability, a visible willingness to end the thread.

## 4. Structure — STRONG serialized

STRONG = Set the frame, Tell the story, Reveal the intrigue, Offer the prize, Nail the hookpoint, Get a decision.

- **Announce brevity up front ("3-minute read", "12 slides").** A declared time-box raises status and holds attention; running long reads as desperation.
- **Section order: (1) frame + why-now, (2) big idea, (3) story/intrigue, (4) secret sauce + headline numbers, (5) the deal, (6) decision step.** Don't lead with team bios or market-size boilerplate.
- **Use the "why now" three-forces pattern early:** what economic, social, and technological shifts just made this possible/urgent. Nobody buys a stale deal; frame a moving opportunity, not a static thing.
- **Use the big-idea template as a drafting check:** "For [target customers], who are dissatisfied with [current offering], my product is a [new category] that provides [key problem solved]. Unlike [competitor], we [key differentiator]." One sentence forces the skim-survivable version of the whole pitch to exist.
- **The deal section states plainly what they get, what you need, and what happens next — a few lines, not a term paper.**
- **End with a decision mechanism and date, not "thoughts welcome."** An ending without a fork trains the reader to do nothing.

## 5. Intrigue and push-pull

- **Embed one narrator-centered intrigue story** — a person, stakes, time pressure, obstacle — and withhold the resolution until later in the doc or the next touch. The open loop carries the reader through the analytical middle.
- **Break tension-then-release rhythm into the copy with push-pull pairs.** "There's a real chance this isn't a fit — but if it is, this could be big." The push proves non-neediness; the pull re-engages. A pitch that is 100% pull reads as chasing.
- **Never resolve every question in the pitch itself; the goal of the pitch is the next meeting, not the close.** Full disclosure invites the analyst frame; intrigue invites a reply.

## 6. Hot vs. cold cognition

- **Sequence for a hot decision:** stack intrigue + prize + time signals *before* analysis; put dense analysis after the ask or in an appendix. People decide via wanting and rationalize afterward; leading with spreadsheets produces a slow, skeptical maybe.
- **Give the cold-cognition reader an appendix so the hot pitch stays clean.** Don't fight the analyst; quarantine them.

## 7. Honest evidence assessment

- The croc-brain framing rests on MacLean's triune-brain model, which modern neuroscience treats as discredited. The practical kernel — attention is scarce, first impressions are fast and affect-driven, complexity loses readers — is consistent with dual-process research, but Klaff cites almost none of it.
- The method's evidence is Klaff's own deal war stories: self-reported and survivorship-biased. No controlled comparisons exist.
- Manufactured time pressure demonstrably *reduces* decision quality; use real deadlines only.
- The frame-busting persona can read as arrogance in writing, where tone lacks live humor's softening. The most defensible, transferable subset is the negative rule set: kill neediness, kill supplication, kill over-explaining, keep it short and concrete, own your own agenda and clock.
