# Modern B2B positioning (April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome*, 2019)

The modern correction to Ries & Trout for B2B/dev-tools. Read together with
`positioning.md`; where they conflict on B2B products, Dunford wins.

## Core thesis: positioning is context-setting

Positioning is deliberately setting the **context** a product is judged in.
Buyers slot a product into a mental category within seconds, then use that
category's assumptions to fill in competitors, pricing, key features, and who
it's for. Her canonical example: the same database product went nowhere as a
"general database" and took off repositioned as a "data warehouse" — nothing
changed but the frame.

**Why feature-first copy fails:** a feature list before a category frame gives
the reader no scaffold — "fast" means nothing until the reader knows fast
*compared to what*.

## The 5 (+1) components — in dependency order

Each component derives from the previous; Dunford starts from **your happiest
customers** and works outward (Ries & Trout start from the market leader):

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what your best customers would actually *do* without you. In B2B usually **"do nothing," a spreadsheet, an intern, a bash script, or the incumbent internal process** — not a named rival.
2. **Unique attributes** — capabilities those *actual* alternatives lack. (Unique vs. the spreadsheet is a different list than unique vs. the funded rival.)
3. **Value and proof** — what the attributes *enable*, backed by objective evidence. Attributes → value is a translation step; unproven value claims are noise.
4. **Target market characteristics** — observable traits of the people who care *intensely* about that value ("who cares a lot"), not the TAM.
5. **Market category** — the frame you deliberately declare because it makes your value obvious to that segment. Category is an output of 1–4, never an input.
6. **(Bonus) Relevant trends** — a tailwind layered on to feel current. Warning: a trend is seasoning, never the dish — **trend without category = confusion** ("AI-powered platform for the future of work").

Writer's compression of her 10-step process: start from fans, not the roadmap
→ drop the baggage (the launch category and founder vision are *default*
positioning, not chosen) → derive alternatives → attributes → value →
who-cares → category, in order → write it down once so every channel
instantiates the same position.

## Three positioning plays — and when each is right

- **Head-to-head** (win the existing category on its own criteria): right when there's no entrenched leader or you out-resource the field. Wrong — and common slop — for a bootstrapped tool declaring war on the gorilla.
- **Big fish, small pond** (subsegment): keep the known category, add a sharp qualifier for a segment whose needs the leader ignores ("observability for edge Workers"). The default play for most B2B/dev-tools products — dense word-of-mouth, winnable ground. Requires the segment's needs to be *truly distinct* and expensive for the leader to copy.
- **Create a new game** (invent the category): hardest and most expensive — you must sell the category before the product, and an educated market can be harvested by a bigger competitor. Right only when the product genuinely fits no existing frame and you can fund the education.

## The sales-narrative ordering

(From her capture step in *Obviously Awesome*, fully developed in *Sales
Pitch*, 2023.) The antidote to feature-first copy:

1. **The insight/problem** — a point of view on what's changed or broken in the buyer's world.
2. **The alternatives and their gap** — the honest map of what people do today (including do-nothing/spreadsheets) and where each falls short. This builds trust and sets the evaluation criteria in your favor.
3. **The perfect world** — the criteria a real solution would meet, stated before your product appears.
4. **Product intro *with category*** — "the [category] that [differentiated value]," then proof.

## Where Dunford corrects Ries & Trout for modern B2B

- **Category choice is the leverage, not "owning a word."** The higher-leverage move is choosing the backdrop itself; the word follows from the frame.
- **Competitive alternatives are behaviors, not brands.** The real rival is usually "do nothing" or a spreadsheet — position against what buyers would actually do, or the differentiation list is fiction.
- **Bottom-up, not leader-relative.** Reason upward from your best customers' behavior; "big fish, small pond" is the creneau made operational — a repeatable derivation instead of a slogan.
- **She rejects the classic template** ("For [target] who [need], X is a [category] that [benefit], unlike [competitor]...") as fill-in-the-blanks on unvalidated inputs. Derive the components first, then compress.
- **Positioning precedes messaging.** You cannot write the homepage until the five components are settled; copywriting is downstream instantiation.
- **Early-stage caveat:** don't lock positioning before consistent customer patterns emerge — let the market pull you. Permanence-of-position framing suits incumbents, not pre-PMF products.

## Rules (imperative + why, channel-tagged)

**Category and frame**

- **Declare the category before any feature or adjective.** [pitch, landing, bio] Category-first copy borrows a whole mental model in three words.
- **Pick the category that makes your strengths the default evaluation criteria — even if it's not the category you started in.** [pitch, landing]
- **If big-fish-small-pond, weld the qualifier to the category — "X for [segment]," never bare "X".** [landing, bio, email] The unqualified category invites comparison with the gorilla on the gorilla's criteria.
- **Don't claim a new category unless the product truly fits no existing frame and you can afford to educate the market.** [pitch, landing]
- **Name at most one relevant trend, and only after the category is unambiguous.** [landing, pitch, bio]

**Alternatives and differentiation**

- **Position against what the reader would actually do without you — usually a spreadsheet, a cron job, or nothing.** [email, landing, pitch] Differentiation vs. an alternative the buyer never considered is invisible.
- **Check every "unique" claim against the true alternatives list; cut any claim the do-nothing option also satisfies.** [landing, email] "Easy to set up" is not a differentiator against doing nothing.
- **In cold email, name the status-quo behavior first ("you're probably stitching this together with cron + S3"), then the gap.** [email] Recognition of their actual current state earns the next sentence.

**Value, proof, and audience**

- **State value as the outcome the attribute enables, then attach proof in the same breath.** [landing, email]
- **Target the segment that cares intensely, via characteristics they self-recognize ("teams shipping to Cloudflare Workers"), not demographics.** [landing, email, bio]
- **Cut any sentence that would survive on a competitor's page unchanged.** [landing, pitch, bio] Interchangeable copy is evidence the components were never worked out.

**Ordering**

- **Never open with features. Order: problem/insight → today's alternatives and their gap → what a real fix looks like → product-as-category → proof.** [pitch, landing, email]
- **Landing hero = "[category] that [differentiated value] for [who]."** [landing] The five-component chain compressed to one testable sentence.
- **Bios and one-liners: category + qualifier + one differentiated value; drop the mission statement.** [pitch, bio] A bio is read in two seconds — the timescale on which readers assign a category; either you assign it or they do.
- **Check all channels against one written positioning doc.** [all] Divergent frames means the buyer meets three different products with one name.

**Checkable tests**

- Can a stranger name your category after the first line?
- Does the copy name the real-world alternative it beats — including do-nothing?
- Is every value claim paired with proof within one sentence?
- Would the ideal buyer recognize themselves in the first two lines?
- Does any feature appear before the frame is set? If yes, reorder.
