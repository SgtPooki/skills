# Direct-response laws (Hopkins, *Scientific Advertising*, 1923; Caples, *Tested Advertising Methods*)

Rules extracted for modern broadcast business writing (pitches, cold email, X
promo, outreach). Imperative + one-line why. Overlapping rules are merged into
Shared laws at the end. Hopkins quotes are verbatim from the public-domain full
text; Caples material is attested across multiple independent summaries.

## Hopkins — Scientific Advertising

1. **Write as a salesman speaking to one buyer, not as a performer seeking applause.** "Advertising is salesmanship... multiplied salesmanship" — the only test of a line is: would you say it to a prospect standing in front of you? Check: read the draft aloud as if to one skeptical customer.
2. **Replace every superlative with a measured fact.** "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck... a man who makes a specific claim is either telling the truth or a lie" — readers discount "best/fastest/leading" as puffery, but "actual figures are not generally discounted." His examples: "gives three and one-third times the light" beats "gives more light." Check: each superlative or unquantified comparative converts to a number, a named mechanism, or is deleted.
3. **Frame everything as service to the reader; never ask them to do you a favor.** "The people you address are selfish, as we all are. They care nothing about your interest or your profit." The best ads "ask no one to buy"; they offer wanted information, an advantage, a sample. Check: count sentences whose subject is you/your company vs. the reader; the reader should win.
4. **Lead with an offer the reader can accept without risk or effort.** Hopkins' winners offered a sample, a free week's trial, "without cost or obligation." Modern form: free tier, live demo link, "reply and I'll send the benchmark."
5. **Use the headline to select your buyer, not to amuse the crowd.** "The purpose of a headline is to pick out people you can interest" — a clever blind headline attracts wrong readers and hides the ad from right ones. Check: could the target reader tell from the first line alone that this is for them?
6. **Never joke, never be eccentric about the sale.** "People do not patronize a clown" — attention won by wearing a fool's cap ruins selling prospects.
7. **Tell the full story once you have attention; don't ration arguments across posts.** "When you once get a person's attention, then is the time to accomplish all you can ever hope with him." Readers decide in one reading and rarely return; teaser campaigns "almost never connect."
8. **Write every message for the new reader, not existing fans.** "In every ad consider only new customers." Assume zero prior context in each broadcast piece.
9. **Narrow the offer to a named class to make it more wanted.** "An offer limited to a certain class of people is far more effective than a general offer." Modern form: "for teams already on Postgres," "first 50 replies."
10. **Sell the desired outcome, not the avoided pain.** "People will do much to cure trouble, but people in general will do little to prevent it... Show a bright side." Check: is the promise a gain the reader pictures, or a lecture about risk?
11. **Don't lead with cheapness.** "Cheapness is not a strong appeal" — discount-first framing cheapens the product.
12. **Test everything traceable; treat untested opinion — including your own — as guesswork.** "No guesswork is permitted... Never be guided in any way by ads which are untraced." Modern form: per-variant links/UTMs, A/B subject lines, reply-rate tracking; kill losers fast.

## Caples — Tested Advertising Methods

1. **Spend most of your effort on the headline (subject line / first X line); it carries the money.** Caples held that the majority of readers read little beyond it, and mediocre copy under an excellent headline beats the reverse. (The oft-quoted "80% never get past the headline" is later copywriter folklore, not a Caples measurement — don't cite it as one.)
2. **Build the headline on one of the four tested qualities, ranked: (1) self-interest, (2) news, (3) curiosity, (4) quick-easy-way.** Self-interest ("Retire at 55") is strongest; curiosity only works fused with one of the others.
3. **His five headline rules:** (a) get self-interest into every headline; (b) if you have genuine news, lead with it; (c) avoid curiosity-only headlines; (d) avoid negative/gloomy headlines; (e) suggest a quick and easy way to get what they want.
4. **Use his tested formula words as historical winners to test, not defaults:** *Announcing, New, Now, At last, How to, Why, Which, Who else, Wanted, Advice*; put a date or price in the headline, feature a free offer, tell a story, use a testimonial, offer the reader a test, address a specific group. Proven examples: "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano — But When I Started to Play!", "Do You Make These Mistakes in English?", "How I Improved My Memory in One Evening." **2026 caveat:** several of these (*Free*, *Act now*, *Announcing*) are spam-filter and pattern-saturation bait in email — in that channel the modern-constraints rules override the formula words.
5. **Long headlines that say something beat short headlines that say nothing.** "It's what you say that counts, not how you say it."
6. **Tested LOSERS — do not use:** clever/witty wordplay, humor, blind curiosity headlines, negative framing, and brag-and-boast copy ("advertisers so full of their own accomplishments that they forget to tell us why we should buy").
7. **Make the first paragraph short and make it cash the headline's check.** A first paragraph that changes subject or warms up loses the reader the headline earned.
8. **Write plainly and mechanically simple:** short paragraphs, short sentences, short words. Write like you talk.
9. **Make claims believable with exact, odd-numbered specifics.** "This course made me $24,385 in the first year" beats "made me a lot of money." Precise figures signal a real measurement.
10. **When you have a real story to tell, long copy outsells short — for interested readers in high-involvement channels.** Interested readers read everything; length only hurts the uninterested, who weren't buying anyway. **Scope limit:** this held for mail-order ads and holds for landing pages and warm long-form; it inverts for cold email (modern benchmarks favor ~50–125 words) and mobile feeds. Use enough copy to answer the buying question — no more.
11. **Test the appeal before the wording.** Caples' famous proof of stakes: one ad "sold 19½ times as much goods as another" in identical space, same care in copy — the difference was the *appeal*, not the prose. A/B the value proposition before polishing body copy.

## Shared laws (both books)

1. **Specificity beats superlatives.** Every claim carries a number, name, or mechanism; generalities are discounted on sight.
2. **Self-interest first — the reader does not care about you.** The piece answers "what do I get?" in the first line.
3. **Clever, funny, and curiosity-for-its-own-sake LOSE.** Wit is for the writer's ego, not the reader's wallet.
4. **The headline's job is everything:** it selects the right reader (Hopkins) and carries ~80% of the result (Caples). Write it last, longest, and in many variants.
5. **Avoid the negative; promise the bright side.**
6. **Make acting easy and low-risk, and give a reason to act now.** Exactly one CTA, zero-friction, with a concrete reason it's better done today.
7. **Test; never trust opinion — including your own.** Every broadcast piece keyed (distinct link/variant) so winners are known, not felt.
8. **Long, complete copy beats teaser brevity — for the interested reader in a channel they control.** For X threads: the hook tweet is the headline; the thread is the full story. For cold email, brevity wins the first touch; the full story goes in the linked writeup the reader opts into.
