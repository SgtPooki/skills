# Collier (*The Robert Collier Letter Book*, 1931)

Collier's distinct contribution vs. Halbert/Sugarman: he wrote **cold letters
to strangers, measured by response rate**, and his system revolves around one
move — joining the reader's existing mental conversation instead of starting
your own. Quotes verified against the full text (public-domain-era edition).
His five-touch sequence data is the skill's follow-up backbone.

## 1. Enter the conversation already going on

The doctrine, in his words: "Every man is constantly holding a mental conversation with himself, the burden of which is his own interests... And you have tried to chime in on that conversation with something that fits in with his thoughts." His negative case: the stranger who interrupts a deep discussion with "See here, Mister, I have a fine coat I want to sell you!" — you'd "kick him and the coat down the nearest stairs."

Rules (cold email: first line / whole framing):

- **Before writing a word, answer: "What is this person arguing with himself about right now?"** The letter that mirrors an active internal debate beats the letter that opens a new topic.
- **Research the role's standing preoccupation, not just the company.** Collier's targeting was occupational: what a gas-plant superintendent thinks about (cost per cubic foot). Cold email: recent launch, hiring spree, outage, migration — whatever their current self-conversation is provably about.
- **Open on a subject already in their head, then bridge "logically to the point you wanted to discuss."** The bridge is mandatory: attention without a logical tie to the offer is the "Fire!"-in-the-saloon trick — fatal to the sale.
- **If their mind offers no natural entry point, use news value** — a genuinely newsworthy fact about the proposition that diverts attention, then ties back. Sanctioned fallback, not the default.
- **Bait is chosen by the fish.** "What is the bait that will tempt your reader?" — cut every line that exists because *you* like your product.
- **Don't burn the list with tin bait.** "You may attract a fish's attention with a gaudily painted bauble, but if he once nibbles it and finds it made of tin, you will have a hard time reaching him again" — a misleading first email poisons all follow-ups.

## 2. The Six Essentials (his exact taxonomy)

"Every good letter contains these six essential elements":

1. **The opening** — fits the reader's train of thought, establishes a point of contact with his interests.
2. **The description or explanation** — pictures the proposition, important features first, then details.
3. **The motive or reason why** — creates longing by describing "not your proposition but what it will do for him."
4. **The proof or guarantee** — evidence for the claims, or a confidence-establishing guarantee.
5. **The snapper or penalty** — immediate action via the specific loss of not acting now.
6. **The close** — "tells the reader just what to do and how to do it, and makes it easy for him to act at once."

Two attached caveats, both checkable:

- **Blend, don't enumerate.** "You may even mix them all up into one grand goulash" — use the six as a review checklist, never as visible section headings.
- **Feeling outranks mechanics.** His A/B evidence: the rule-following letter was moderately successful; its rival "literally pulled its head off... because the first letter was aimed only at the intellect, whereas the second... aimed its real appeal at the emotions."

## 3. The opening = continuation of their train of thought

- **The reader is "a man in a speeding train"** — one glance; the first line must catch the eye AND survive inspection, "once lost, it is twice as hard to win the second time." Subject + preview text are the glance; sentence one must pay them off.
- **Write the opener as the reader's own question, voiced back.** His model: "What is the eternal question... 'How can I better my condition?'" State their problem as they would phrase it, before you exist in the email.
- **He is not looking for your letter.** Never open with your name, company, or "I'm reaching out" — lead with the side of the story the reader cares about, like "the newspaper paragrapher."

## 4. Motive — sell what it does for him

- **Six prime motives: love, gain, duty, pride, self-indulgence, self-preservation.** Pick the dominant one before drafting; reinforce with secondaries.
- **Description never sells: "It is what it will do for the one who buys it that counts!"**
- **Distinguish the desire motive from the action motive.** The reason they'd want the outcome and the reason they'd reply this week are different sentences — write both.
- **In B2B, gain beats claimed altruism.** "We are much readier to believe that some one is unselfishly interested in helping us to make or save money" than in our general welfare — pure-altruism framing reads false.

## 5. Word-pictures — make him live the outcome

- **"The mind thinks in pictures... the reader colors that picture with his own imagination."** One concrete scene beats three abstract benefit claims.
- **Build the picture on something already familiar** — describe the outcome in terms of a tool, metric, or moment the prospect already knows (their dashboard, their Monday standup).
- **Describe in the reader's dialect** — mirror the vocabulary of their job posting, their docs, their talk; not yours.
- **Assemble like a house: framework first, then details** — never "a series of firstlys, and secondlys... putting your reader to sleep." No bulleted feature lists in a first touch.
- **"Your sale must be made in your reader's mind"** — before the ask, the reader must have already experienced the result.

## 6. The snapper — the reason to act now

- **Two levers only: desire strong enough to reach for the pen, or fear of losing something worthwhile.**
- **"When you want to inspire fear, be definite! Be specific!"** — an exact date, an exact quantity ("Only 46 sets left!"), an exact price change. Vague urgency carries no conviction.
- **Give the deadline a reason and a mechanism.** His device: a numbered, non-transferable certificate "not good after ten days" — believable because explained. "Two pilot slots for Q4" only works if the reader can see why the limit exists.
- **Prefer lowering the stakes over raising the pressure.** "Don't decide now... Try it out... Then decide" — find "some easy preliminary task" that keeps him moving. The cold-email ask is a micro-commitment ("worth a look?"), never "buy."

## 7. Tested letters — what made winners win

- **Harvard Classics to dead leads: 4.5% orders on a $39 set from 10,000 stale inquiries salesmen had abandoned.** Borrowed-authority scene opener ("If Dr. Eliot of Harvard were to say to you..."), shrunk commitment ("just 15 minutes a day"), zero-risk trial ("Don't send a cent... keep them for a week"). Lesson: authority + shrink-the-commitment + risk-free trial revives cold lists.
- **Powellton coal: reframed the unit of comparison.** Instead of price per ton like every rival, his letter asked gas companies "How much are you paying per cubic foot for your gas? If we can show you how to cut the cost by a fourth, are you interested enough to prove it?" — then quoted exact figures from a named customer. Lessons: change the metric to one where you win; open with a question in the buyer's own P&L; proof = named peer + exact numbers. A near-perfect cold-email template.
- **Black raincoats to undertakers: 10,000 letters sold out three production runs.** The offer came from one customer's stated problem generalized to the occupation; as he scaled, "he never sent the same letter to undertakers as to doctors or ministers," and quoted in-segment testimonials. Lesson: segment by role, one letter per segment, in-segment social proof.
- **Envelope-level variables moved measurable response** (circulars +25–33%, letterhead +10–20%). For email: test sender name, subject, and snippet — not just body copy.

## 8. Follow-up sequences (measured)

- **A sequence outpulls any single letter — plan five touches.** His measured series: 4.5% → 2.5% → 1.5% → 1% → **2% on the final letter** (the last-chance rebound). Sixth-and-later follow-ups were "practically fruitless" — #5 is the mopping-up letter, then stop.
- **Vary the format and angle each touch, never repeat the pitch.** #4 was a recapitulating folder; #5 was a shock of contrast (four dense pages) — the contrast doubled response off the floor. Each follow-up changes the vehicle; never "just bumping this."
- **Each new appeal harvests a different sub-audience.** Rotate motive (gain → pride → self-preservation) and hook across the sequence — non-responders to appeal A aren't dead, they're mis-baited. His warning: "the unpardonable sin of stagnation."
- **Follow up while interest is warm, with an expiring device** — his standard action window was ten days, enforced by an explained, non-transferable deadline.
- **Customers are the cheapest list.** First sale may cost 10–50% of price; the second "should seldom cost more than 5 or 10 percent" — cold acquisition justifies itself by the follow-on list it builds.

## Cold-email checklist distilled from Collier

1. First line joins a conversation the prospect is already having with himself (verifiable from research), then bridges logically to the offer.
2. Zero sentences about you before the reader's problem is on the page in his own words.
3. One dominant motive chosen deliberately (gain is the B2B default), phrased as what he gets.
4. One concrete word-picture of the after-state, built on something he already knows.
5. Proof = exact numbers + a named peer in the same occupation.
6. A reason-why deadline or scarce slot, specific and mechanically believable — plus a lowered-stakes micro-ask.
7. Close dictates the exact next action ("we are all mentally lazy... dictate his action for him").
8. Five-touch sequence, each touch a new format and a new motive; stop after the mopping-up email.
