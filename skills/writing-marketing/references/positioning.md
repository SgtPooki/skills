# Positioning (Ries & Trout, *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind*, 1981)

Checkable rules for broadcast business writing, extracted for a solo developer /
small product context. Each rule: imperative + one-line why, then per-channel
application (one-line pitch, X/Twitter bio & promo, cold email).

## 1. Write for the mind the reader already has, not the product you built

**Rule: Name a category the reader already knows before saying anything new.**
Why: "Positioning is not what you do to a product; it's what you do to the mind of the prospect" — new information only sticks if it hooks onto an existing mental slot.

- **Pitch:** Lead with a known category, then your twist: "X for Y" or "A [known thing] that [one difference]." Check: could a stranger repeat your category back after one read?
- **Bio:** First clause = category, in the reader's vocabulary, not yours ("terminal file manager", not "reimagined workspace paradigm").
- **Cold email:** Sentence one anchors to something the recipient already uses or suffers from ("You're probably running X for this today"). Never open with your product's name plus adjectives.

**Rule: Ban inside-out language — audit every draft for words only you care about.**
Why: positioning is thinking in reverse — start with the mind of the prospect; your architecture, your journey, and your feature names are inside-out thinking.
Check: strike any sentence whose subject is "we/I/the product" that could not be rewritten with "you" as the subject. Strike internal jargon unless the reader searched for it.

## 2. Own one word

**Rule: Pick one word or two-word attribute your product owns; every broadcast asset must contain it and no rival claim.**
Why: the mind assigns one attribute per brand (Volvo = safety, FedEx = overnight); claiming two dilutes both, claiming five owns nothing.

- **Pitch:** One claim only. "Fastest," or "offline," or "zero-config" — never "fast, private, and easy." Check: count the differentiating claims; more than one = fail.
- **Bio:** Category + your one word + who it's for. Cut everything else. A bio listing four benefits is filed by the mind as nothing.
- **Cold email:** State your one word in the first two sentences, before any feature list. Features appear only as evidence for that single word, never as parallel claims.

**Rule: Don't claim a word a leader already owns.**
Why: "If your proposed position calls for a head-on approach against a marketing leader, forget it" — the same claim from a smaller player only advertises the leader.
Check: if the incumbent's homepage could run your headline unchanged, rewrite. "Fast search" when Algolia exists is Algolia's ad, paid by you.

## 3. Use the ladder: be first, be against, or find a creneau

**Rule: If you can't be first in an existing category, invent a narrower category where you are first.**
Why: the mind ranks a short ladder per category and the top rung gets the business; a new ladder makes you rung one by definition.

- **Pitch:** Narrow until first is true: not "a monitoring tool" but "the only uptime monitor that runs on your own router." Check: is "first/only" literally defensible for the category as stated?
- **Bio:** "The only X that Y" beats "a better X." If you can't write "only," your category is too broad.
- **Cold email:** Frame the niche so the incumbent is out of frame: "Datadog-class alerts for people with one server and no budget" creates a ladder Datadog isn't on.

**Rule: If you must share a ladder with a leader, position explicitly against them — relate to their rung, exploit their structural weakness.**
Why: Avis's "We're No. 2. We try harder" worked by borrowing Hertz's position; repositioning changes the prospect's mind about the competitor, not about you.

- **Pitch:** Name the relation: "Everything you use Notion for, without the sync lag." The leader's name is the hook; their structural weakness (bigness, price, cloud-dependence, complexity) is the wedge.
- **Promo post:** Attack what the leader *cannot* fix without abandoning their position (their cloud, their pricing model, their enterprise focus) — never a feature they can ship next quarter.
- **Cold email:** "You're likely on [leader]. Here's the one case where it fails you: ___." Repositions them and positions you in two sentences.
- Check: an honest underdog admission ("we're small, so ___") is a legal, credible move; a claim to beat the leader at their own word is not.

**Rule: Hunt for an uncontested creneau (size, price, timing, user segment, distribution) and state it literally.**
Why: followers win by filling holes the leader leaves open (Nyquil owned "nighttime"), not by being a slightly better everything.
Check: your copy names a segment or context the leader ignores ("for solo devs," "for 2am incidents") — if the audience clause fits everyone, it's not a creneau.

## 4. Refuse the everybody trap and the line-extension trap

**Rule: Exclude someone by name in every positioning statement.**
Why: the company that is everything to everybody ends up nothing to anybody — a too-broad position can't be established in the prospect's mind.

- **Pitch/Bio:** Include "for [specific someone]" and accept that it repels others; that repulsion is the mechanism working. Check: can you name a plausible customer your copy correctly turns away? If not, it's too broad.
- **Cold email:** One recipient persona per template. An email that would read fine to both a CTO and a hobbyist persuades neither.

**Rule: One product, one name, one position — don't stretch a name that stands for something onto a second thing.**
Why: line extensions blur the position occupied by the original name; a name that comes to mean two things means neither.

- Solo-dev translation: don't promote your CLI, your SaaS, and your course under one brand voice in one bio or thread. Give the new thing its own name and its own one word, or don't broadcast it yet.
- Check: does your bio/pitch describe more than one product? Cut to the one you're currently positioning.

## 5. Oversimplify — the mind hates confusion

**Rule: Cut the message to one oversimplified idea and repeat it unchanged everywhere.**
Why: so little of any message gets through that only a sharpened, obvious claim survives the noise — and consistency over time is what lodges it.

- **Pitch:** One sentence, one clause of qualification max. If it needs a second sentence to be understood, the position is wrong, not the wording.
- **Promo posts:** Same one word, same category framing, every post. Novelty in examples, never in the claim. Check: your last five posts should all support the identical claim.
- **Cold email:** One ask, one value prop, under 120 words. Every added feature paragraph lowers the odds the one idea lands.

**Rule: When your positioning feels embarrassingly obvious, ship it.**
Why: the mind admires the complicated and dismisses the obvious — clever copy is usually inside-out copy.
Check: if you're proud of the wordplay, rewrite plainly. If it sounds too simple to you but instantly clear to an outsider, it's done.

## 6. Make the name do positioning work

**Rule: Choose (and use) a name that hints at the category — almost generic, not quite.**
Why: the name is the hook the position hangs on; a name that telegraphs the category does half the positioning for free ("filecoin-pin" beats "Zephyr").

- Check: does a stranger guess roughly what it does from the name alone? Coined mean-nothing names are only affordable if you're first in a genuinely new category.
- **Bio/Promo:** If the name doesn't self-explain, never let it appear without its category apposition ("Zephyr — offline CI for laptops"). Avoid initialisms; an unknown brand shortened to letters is a position thrown away.

## 7. The don't list (each is a checkable failure)

- **Don't** make the same claim as the leader with a "better" in front — better doesn't dislodge first.
- **Don't** position from your own perspective — no headline whose real audience is you, your investors, or Hacker News commenters.
- **Don't** stack claims — two differentiators in one sentence = zero owned words.
- **Don't** chase everyone — copy with no excluded audience is copy with no audience.
- **Don't** line-extend your name across products in broadcast channels.
- **Don't** change your one word between the bio, the pitch, and the email — inconsistency resets the position to zero.
- **Don't** be clever where you can be clear — the mind rejects what it must decode.

## Composite templates

- **One-line pitch:** `The only [narrow category] that [one word/claim], for [excluded-audience-implying someone].`
- **Twitter bio:** `[Category in reader's words] · [one owned word] · for [specific someone]` — nothing else.
- **Cold email value prop:** `You use/suffer [thing in their head] → here's the one place it fails you → [product] is [category] that [one word]. [Single feature as proof.] [One ask.]`
