# Ogilvy (*Confessions of an Advertising Man*, 1963; *Ogilvy on Advertising*, 1983)

What Ogilvy adds beyond Hopkins/Caples (the skill's direct-response base).
Rules tagged **[ADDS]** (genuinely new) or **[CORROBORATES]** (reinforces the
existing base). Attribution caveats at the end.

## 1. Do the homework before writing a word

- **[ADDS] Study the product until you find its inherent drama — do not draft before you have a Big Idea.** "The more you know, the more likely you are to have a big idea." He spent three weeks reading about Rolls-Royce before finding "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise... comes from the electric clock" — a line lifted from a technical editor's review, not invented. The strongest claim is usually already in the material; invention is what you do when you skipped the reading. *Checkable: can you point to the source document/fact the headline came from?*
- **[ADDS] The Big-Idea test battery:** Did it make me gasp? Do I wish I'd thought of it? Is it unique? Does it fit the strategy exactly? Could it run for 30 years? Filters cleverness-of-the-week from durable positioning.
- **[CORROBORATES — Hopkins] Steal from the customer's own language.** He built ads from research interviews and product literature; the Rolls headline was a quotation.

## 2. Respect the reader

- **[ADDS] "The consumer isn't a moron; she is your wife."** Operational form: never write anything you'd be embarrassed to say to one intelligent person face-to-face — no shouting, no insulting simplification, no tricks that assume the reader won't notice. *Checkable: read the draft aloud as if to one specific competent adult.*
- **[ADDS] "Tell the truth, but make the truth fascinating."** Dullness is a failure of craft, not a badge of honesty — "You cannot bore people into buying your product." *Checkable: is every sentence both true and interesting, or is one being sacrificed for the other?*
- **[CORROBORATES — Caples] Give the reader useful information; the ad should be worth reading even if they don't buy.**

## 3. Brand image compounds

- **[ADDS] "Every advertisement is part of the long-term investment in the personality of the brand."** Before publishing, ask: does this piece deposit into or withdraw from the personality we want in 10 years? Discount-shouting wins the click and cheapens the brand. *Checkable: would this post be on-voice if it ran unchanged for five years?*
- **[ADDS] Brand + direct response coexist: measure like a direct marketer, sound like the brand.** Ogilvy held both — direct-response discipline ("my first love and secret weapon") while insisting every response ad stay in brand character. Sloppy tone is just an unmeasured cost.

## 4. Headlines — the exact data

- **[CORROBORATES Caples, ADDS the stat] "On the average, five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. When you have written your headline, you have spent eighty cents out of your dollar."** This is the accurate form of the headline-dominance claim (the "80% never read past it" version is folklore).
- **[ADDS] Put the brand/product name and the promise in the headline itself** — 4 out of 5 readers see nothing else.
- **[CORROBORATES] Promise a benefit; news and specifics outperform cleverness.** His readership data: long headlines carrying news and price sell more than short vague ones — length is fine when every word is information.
- **[ADDS] Never run a "blind" headline that only makes sense after reading the body.** Most readers never read the body.

## 5. Long copy, factual copy

- **[CORROBORATES Hopkins, sharpens] "The more you tell, the more you sell" — for considered, high-involvement purchases.** Body copy is read by roughly 1 reader in 10, but that 10% are the prospects — write to them, at length, with facts (the Rolls ad ran 719 words of specifics).
- **[ADDS] Write copy as a sequence of facts, not adjectives.** *Checkable: strike every sentence containing no fact; what survives is the ad.*
- **[ADDS] Sub-headlines every few paragraphs, second person, short first sentences.** Long copy only works when scannable and personal.

## 6. Anti-cleverness

- **[ADDS] "If it doesn't sell, it isn't creative."** (Popularized form of his longer sentence — cite as "attributed".) Kill any line whose main achievement is that the writer enjoyed it. *Checkable: does the cleverest sentence in the draft carry a selling fact? If not, cut it.*
- **[ADDS] The Demosthenes test:** you want the reader to say "I never knew that. I must try this product" — not "what clever advertising." (His Aeschines/Demosthenes passage: "When Demosthenes spoke, they said 'Let us march against Philip.'")

## 7. Layout rules that transfer to landing pages

- **[ADDS] Image at top, headline under it, copy under the headline** — match the reader's natural scanning order; a headline above the image forces backtracking.
- **[ADDS] Caption every image, and make the caption a miniature ad** (name + promise) — captions are read by more people than body copy. Landing-page translation: figure captions and image overlays are prime selling real estate.
- **[ADDS] Never set body copy in reverse type (white on dark) or over a tinted/image background** — it measurably kills readership (Save the Children doubled revenue switching to black-on-white). Dark heroes are fine for headlines; long copy goes dark-on-light.
- **[ADDS] Photographs of the result (before/after) and images with story appeal (the Hathaway eyepatch) out-pull abstract art.** *Checkable: does the image make the viewer ask a question?*

## 8. First paragraph and story appeal

- **[ADDS] The first paragraph is a short, gripping continuation of the headline's promise — never a warm-up.** *Checkable: delete paragraph one — if the ad improves, it was throat-clearing.*
- **[ADDS] Inject story:** "The amazing story of a Zippo that worked after being taken from the belly of a fish." Narrative sustains long copy that a spec list cannot.

## 9. Committees, awards, puffery

- **[ADDS] Don't write by committee:** "Search all your parks in all your cities; you'll find no statues of committees." One accountable author; committees may veto facts, never wordsmith — committee edits sand off the specific and the daring.
- **[ADDS] Don't optimize for peer applause:** award-chasing "seduces" agencies into ads that win juries and lose customers. Modern translation: engagement from fellow founders/marketers is the awards circuit; optimize for the buyer's action.
- **[CORROBORATES Hopkins] Ban puffery outright:** "superlatives, generalizations and platitudes... lead the reader to discount everything you say." Brag-and-boast is the mark of the amateur.

## Compact checklist (Ogilvy's deltas only)

1. Research artifact exists before drafting; headline traceable to a source fact.
2. Big-Idea test: unique + on-strategy + could run for years.
3. One-intelligent-reader read-aloud test.
4. Brand-personality check: on-voice in five years?
5. Name + promise in the headline; no blind headlines.
6. Every body sentence carries a verifiable fact.
7. Cleverest line must also sell, or it dies.
8. Image → headline → copy order; captioned images; no reversed body type.
9. First paragraph survives the delete test.
10. Single author; optimize for buyer action, not peer applause.

## Attribution caveats

The 5x/eighty-cents stat, the not-a-moron line, the brand-investment line, and
the statues-of-committees line are solidly attributed. "If it doesn't sell, it
isn't creative" is Ogilvy doctrine but the pithy form is a popularization —
quote it as "attributed".
