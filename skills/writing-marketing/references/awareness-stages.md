# Awareness & sophistication (Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966)

The targeting backbone of the skill: which opening a reader can accept depends
on what they already know (awareness) and how many similar claims they've heard
(sophistication). Phrased as checkable imperatives.

## 1. Mass desire: channel it, don't create it

Schwartz's foundation: "Copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of millions of people, and focus those already-existing desires onto a particular product."

- **Never open by arguing the reader should want something.** If you're educating someone into wanting, you've picked the wrong audience or the wrong desire.
- **Before writing a word, name the pre-existing desire (or fear) your product rides on** — one sentence, in the prospect's own words. The desire is the subject of the copy; the product is the vehicle.
- **Pick exactly one desire for the headline/opening.** Two desires in one hook splits attention and halves stopping power.
- **Rank candidate desires by (a) urgency/intensity, (b) staying power, (c) scope.** Lead with the winner; use the rest as body reinforcement.
- **Check:** delete your product name from the opening. Does the sentence still describe something the reader already wants or fears? If it only makes sense with the product in it, you're creating desire, not channeling it.

## 2. The five stages of customer awareness

The single-sentence rule: **your opening must begin at the exact stage of awareness the prospect is in — never ahead of it.**

(Numbering note: Schwartz himself numbered from most-aware downward; this file
uses journey order, unaware = stage 1. Content is identical — but expect the
reversed numbering when cross-reading Schwartz summaries.)

### Stage 5 — Most aware (knows your product, wants it, hasn't bought)
- Must do: lead with product name + the deal — price, discount, deadline, new terms. Archetype: *"Revere Zomar Lens... now only $119.95."*
- Must NOT do: re-explain the problem, re-pitch benefits, warm up. Every word before the offer delays a decision already half-made.
- Check: product name and concrete offer in the first line.

### Stage 4 — Product aware (knows your product, isn't convinced)
- Must do: sharpen superiority — new proof (*"9 out of 10 screen stars use Lux Toilet Soap"*), a new mechanism or improvement, a removed limitation ("now works even if..."), or repositioning against a named alternative.
- Must NOT do: repeat the generic claim they've already seen and discounted.
- Check: the opening contains something the prospect does not already know about the product.

### Stage 3 — Solution aware (wants the result, doesn't know your product)
- Must do: name the desire/result in the headline, prove the result is achievable, then show your product as the mechanism — in that order.
- Must NOT do: lead with your product or company name. Your name means nothing yet; the result means everything.
- Check: first line states the outcome; product name appears only after the outcome is on the table.

### Stage 2 — Problem aware (feels the pain, doesn't know solutions exist)
- Must do: name the problem in the reader's own words and dramatize it — vivid and specific — then introduce the product as the inevitable solution.
- Must NOT do: name the solution category or product in the hook, or assume they know a fix exists.
- Check: could the target reader say "that's exactly my situation" after the first two sentences, before any product is mentioned?

### Stage 1 — Unaware (doesn't recognize or won't admit the desire)
- Must do: open with **identification** — echo the reader's state of mind, their world, a story or curiosity that is about *them*. Archetype: *"Why Men Crack..."* The product enters late.
- Must NOT do: mention product, price, or any direct claim in the opening. There is no desire to attach a claim to yet.
- Check: zero product/price/claim words in the hook; the hook is entirely about the reader or their world.

Cross-stage rules:

- **Never write one message for mixed stages; segment or pick the dominant stage.** An opening calibrated to one stage actively repels the others.
- **When unsure between two stages, write to the less-aware one.** Over-assuming awareness loses the reader entirely; under-assuming only costs a sentence.
- **The headline's only job is to get the first sentence read; each sentence's only job is to get the next one read.** Cut anything that doesn't do that.

## 3. The five stages of market sophistication

Sophistication = how many similar claims the market has already heard. **Diagnose it by counting competitors' promises, not by studying your product.**

1. **Virgin market (no one has made the claim):** state the claim simply and directly. *"Now! Lose Ugly Fat!"* A fresh claim needs no ornament.
2. **Second to market:** keep the claim, enlarge it — bigger number, faster time: *"Lose up to 47 pounds in 4 weeks."* Stop before absurdity; when enlargement stops being believable, the market is at stage 3.
3. **Jaded market (claims heard and discounted):** introduce a **new mechanism** — lead with HOW, not WHAT. *"Floats Fat Right Out of Your Body!"* Prospects no longer ask "will it?" but "how could it possibly?" Check: does your hook contain a named mechanism ("the X method", "via Y") rather than a bare outcome?
4. **Mechanism competition:** elaborate the mechanism — easier, quicker, surer; remove old limitations. *"First No-Diet Reducing Wonder Drug!"*
5. **Exhausted market (all claims distrusted):** abandon claims; sell **identification** — identity, belonging, the kind of person who uses this. Archetype: the Marlboro Man. Note the loop: stage-5 sophistication copy looks like stage-1 awareness copy.

Cross-stage rule: never run a stage-1 bare claim into a stage-3+ market ("Grow your revenue!" to founders who get 40 such emails a week) — it doesn't just underperform, it marks you as spam.

## 4. Intensification: make the chosen claim vivid

Schwartz's second engine — the same claim, made more real:

1. Present the result fully the first time — complete, concrete detail, not an adjective.
2. Put the claim in action — show the product doing the thing, step by step.
3. Put the reader in the scene (future-pace) — the moment after it worked, second person, sensory specifics: "Monday morning, the report is already in your inbox."
4. Show a test the reader could run — let them mentally verify.
5. Stretch benefits across time — day 1, week 2, month 3.
6. Bring in other audiences — the result through colleagues' or the CFO's eyes.
7. Show expert approval — named authority, specific credential.
8. Compare against the named alternative or status quo, on one concrete axis.
9. Picture the negative vividly before the solution — hours, dollars, moments — then relieve it.
10. Stress ease — how little the reader must do.
11. Use one sharp metaphor instead of three abstractions.
12. Summarize by piling desire on desire (the catalog close).
13. Make the guarantee the climactic proof.

Concretization checks for every draft:

- **Replace every abstract benefit word ("efficient," "powerful," "seamless") with a number, a time, a scene, or a named thing.** Abstractions are claims; specifics are evidence.
- **Every claim must be measurable or picturable.** If the reader can't count it or see it, rewrite it.
- **One quantified claim beats three unquantified ones.** Cut the weakest two.

## 5. Gradualization: the belief chain

Schwartz: build a bridge of belief "between those facts as they exist in your prospect's mind today — and the ultimate facts your prospect must believe if he is to accept your claims," starting on the prospect's side.

- **Start from a statement the reader already believes** — verifiably, in their world, in their words. Assent to sentence one buys a hearing for sentence two.
- **List the beliefs the reader must hold to say yes; sequence the copy so each is established before it's relied on.** Write the belief chain as a numbered list before drafting.
- **Never contradict an existing belief head-on; extend it.** Reframe "yet another tool" into "the reason the last three tools failed you." Contradiction triggers defense; extension triggers curiosity.
- **Each step must be small enough that assent is automatic.** If a sentence makes a skeptic say "wait, how?", answer immediately or move it later.
- **Check:** read as the most skeptical plausible prospect and mark the first sentence you'd disbelieve. Everything after that mark is wasted; fix the chain there.

## 6. Awareness stages mapped to modern channels

| Channel | Typical stage | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Cold email / cold DM | Problem-aware at best; often unaware of the solution category | Problem-first opening; no product name in the subject |
| X/Twitter promo posts | Unaware / problem-aware strangers scrolling | Identification or problem-dramatization hooks; product only late in the thread |
| Warm outreach (they engaged, mutual intro) | Solution-aware | Name the result, then the mechanism |
| Solicited pitch deck / booked demo | Solution- to product-aware | Skip problem education fast; spend the time on mechanism, proof, differentiation |
| Newsletter to existing users / renewal | Product- to most-aware | New proof, removed limitation, or straight offer + deadline |

Channel-specific rules:

- **Cold email:** subject and first line are problem-aware moves — name the reader's specific pain, never your product. In a saturated vertical assume sophistication 3+: lead with a mechanism or removed limitation, never the bare outcome ("book more meetings").
- **X promo:** hooks are stage-1/2 territory — open with identification or dramatized problem, never "I built X". Structure threads as a belief chain; the product appears only after problem and mechanism have landed. One concrete artifact (number, screenshot, before/after) per post — the feed is a stage-5-sophistication environment.
- **Solicited pitch:** they invited you, so solution-aware minimum — cap problem framing at one slide/paragraph, spend everything on mechanism, proof, and differentiation vs. the alternatives they're weighing. Re-educating a solution-aware audience on their own problem reads as padding.
- **Follow-ups:** each touch should move the reader one awareness stage, and the copy must change stage with them. "Just following up" is a most-aware move sent to a problem-aware reader — send a new proof point or sharper dramatization instead.

## Master checklist (any broadcast piece)

1. Named the one mass desire, in the prospect's words?
2. Diagnosed the reader's awareness stage and opened exactly there — no product name before the stage permits it?
3. Diagnosed market sophistication by competitors' claims and matched the claim style (bare claim / bigger claim / mechanism / elaborated mechanism / identification)?
4. Every benefit concretized — number, time, scene, or named thing; reader future-paced into the result at least once?
5. Belief chain written out; first-disbelief point found and fixed?
6. Every sentence's only job is to get the next one read — cut the rest?
