# Correction layer: evidence-based marketing (Byron Sharp, *How Brands Grow*, 2010; Romaniuk & Sharp, Part 2, 2016)

Empirical laws from decades of Ehrenberg-Bass buyer-panel data. This layer
corrects — without deleting — the differentiation/positioning/direct-response
doctrine the rest of the skill teaches. Each section flags the **TENSION
WITH** the classic it contradicts. Read the caution section before applying:
the data base is B2C FMCG, and over-correcting is its own failure mode.

**Rule of thumb:** when Sharp and the classics conflict, Sharp wins on *where
to spend attention* (breadth, consistency, light buyers, entry points); the
classics win on *what a single piece of conversion copy must do* (clarity,
offer, proof, one message).

## 1. Mental + physical availability are the growth drivers

- Make the brand easy to think of and easy to buy. For a dev tool, physical availability = install in one command, works without signup, first result on the obvious search, linked wherever the problem is discussed.
- Treat most copy as a salience-building act, not a persuasion act — the buyer usually isn't weighing arguments; they reach for whatever comes to mind first that they can get easily.
- TENSION WITH: Hopkins/Schwartz, which model every exposure as a persuasion event ("salesmanship in print"). Sharp: advertising mostly works by refreshing memory structures. Both are true at different funnel positions — the classic canon over-weights persuasion.

## 2. Distinctive assets over (only) differentiation

- Build and ruthlessly repeat distinctive brand assets: name, logo, color, tagline, characteristic phrasing/format. Their job is recognition ("that's X again"), not argument ("X is better because...").
- Don't force a USP where none exists. Sharp's data: perceived meaningful differentiation among a brand's buyers is low (typically ~10% or less endorse "different/unique"), roughly equal across competing brands, and weakly linked to growth.
- Distinctiveness is legally defensible and durable; differentiation claims are copyable and mostly unnoticed.
- TENSION WITH: Ries & Trout and the USP tradition ("own a word", "differentiate or die"). Sharp's reply: own memory structures and buying situations, not an attribute claim. Counterweight (Ritson): some relative differentiation still matters — treat it as both/and with the budget tilted toward distinctiveness.

## 3. Attach the brand to category entry points (CEPs)

- Map the situations that make someone reach for the category (Romaniuk's W's: when, where, while doing what, with whom, why, feeling what). Link the brand to as many CEPs as it can credibly serve.
- Breadth of mental links beats depth of a single association — big brands are thought of in more situations, not loved more intensely.
- Write different pieces for different CEPs: one message per execution, many executions across entry points.
- TENSION WITH: positioning doctrine's "one narrow position, sacrifice everything else." A single owned position is a single entry point; brands grow by multiplying entry points.

## 4. Penetration over loyalty (double jeopardy)

- Grow by acquiring more buyers, not extracting more from existing ones. Brands differ enormously in penetration, only slightly in loyalty.
- Double jeopardy: small brands have fewer buyers who are also slightly less loyal. Loyalty rises as a mechanical consequence of size, not a lever you pull independently.
- TENSION WITH: retention-first doctrine ("5x cheaper to retain", loyalty ladders, superfan cultivation) and direct response's fixation on the existing list. Sharp calls targeting loyalists for efficiency "a near complete dead-end."

## 5. Write for light buyers — they are most of your future sales

- Assume the median reader barely knows you and thinks about your category rarely. Marketing's Pareto is ~60/20, not 80/20: the top 20% of buyers deliver only about half of sales; the bottom 80% deliver the other half and contain all the growth.
- Never write only for the fans. In-jokes, lore, and advanced-user framing please the already-convinced while excluding the light and non-buyers who determine growth. Every post should work for someone seeing the brand for roughly the first time.
- TENSION WITH: awareness-stage copy optimized for the hottest prospects, and "1000 true fans" community doctrine. Heavy buyers are already buying you.

## 6. Reach over targeting narrowness

- Reach all category buyers; err broad. Tight targeting caps growth by systematically excluding the light and future buyers you can't yet identify.
- Targeting the category is fine (people who ship software); micro-personas within it ("senior Rust devs at seed-stage startups") mostly shrink reach for no lift.
- TENSION WITH: direct response and performance doctrine (segment tightly, personalize, measure per-conversion) and positioning's "pick your niche." Counterweight: for a solo dev the *category itself* is the niche — Sharp argues against narrowing within your category, not against serving a narrow category.

## 7. Consistency over campaign novelty

- Keep the assets, voice, tagline, and visual system stable for years. Wear-out is mostly marketer boredom; audiences see a tiny fraction of what you publish.
- Vary the surface (fresh executions, different CEPs), never the identity. Continuous presence beats launch spikes — memories decay, and buyers enter the market on their own schedule.
- TENSION WITH: big-idea/breakthrough-campaign culture. Test executions; don't churn brand assets.

## 8. Application: solo dev on X + launch copy

- Show up consistently (cadence beats virality) with the same recognizable name, avatar, colors, and catchphrases — light followers accumulate memory links even from skimmed posts.
- Anchor posts to category entry points: "when your CI is slow," "when IPFS pinning breaks" — situation first, brand attached, one CEP per post.
- Put the brand early and often; a post that entertains but is unattributable builds nothing.
- Make it trivially buyable at the moment of mental availability: install command, link, and demo in the post itself.
- Don't over-invest in persuading the already-convinced: fewer superiority threads in the replies, more broad-reach posts putting the brand in a new buying situation.
- Launch copy: treat the launch as one spike in a permanent presence, not the event.

## CAUTION — don't over-correct

- **Data base is B2C FMCG** (soap, soda, banks). High-consideration, few-buyer markets — enterprise B2B, niche dev tools — are less tested; B2B buyer needs vary more, and network-effect dynamics aren't in his data.
- **Differentiation isn't dead** — Sharp shows it is rare and small, not worthless. For a genuinely novel product (common in dev tools), explaining what it does differently is table stakes.
- **Category definition breaks down for niches** — a solo dev's tiny category may be reachable precisely through the "narrow" channels Sharp disparages; there, tight targeting and mass marketing coincide.
- **B2B adaptations temper the numbers** — LinkedIn B2B Institute's 95:5 rule (~95% of the category out-of-market at any time) supports the reach-and-memory logic but keeps real weight on activation/persuasion copy (brand/activation ≈ 46:54 for B2B vs 60:40 B2C).
- **Treat the numbers (60/20, 95:5) as strategic lenses, not constants**; the 2010 core predates algorithmic distribution.
- **Loyalty isn't irrelevant for products with churn** — double jeopardy is about marketing-spend priorities; SaaS retention is a product problem Sharp's framework doesn't address.
